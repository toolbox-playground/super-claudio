# Voiceover — ElevenLabs, validated settings

The narration is generated by ElevenLabs from a voice the user cloned from their own
recordings. This is the route that worked; `open-source-alternative.md` records why the
free local route was abandoned, with measurements.

## Validated configuration

Tuned by a creator against their own Professional Voice Clone and approved by ear, on
pt-BR course narration. Treat them as the default and change one at a time.

| Setting | Value | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Model | **Eleven v4** (`eleven_v4`) | 10,000 characters per request, 90+ languages, the most faithful cloning |
| Voice | **the creator's own clone — ask, never guess** | the one setting the skill cannot default |
| **Similarity** | **100% (High)** | maximum fidelity to the cloned timbre — this is the point |
| **Stability** | **75%** | consistent delivery across dozens of lessons |
| **Clone Strength** | **10%** | PVC + v4 only; see below — this one is counter-intuitive |
| Language | Auto | detects the script's language correctly for pt-BR |
| Output | **MP3, 44.1 kHz** | what the video pipeline loads |
| Audio effects | none | any effect changes the timbre that similarity 100% is preserving |

**Eleven v4 has only Stability and Similarity** — plus Clone Strength when the voice is
a Professional Voice Clone. **There is no Speed and no Style slider in v4, and SSML is
not supported.** If delivery drags or rushes, fix it in the punctuation
(`narration-script.md`), because there is no speed knob to reach for.

**Similarity at 100% is deliberate.** Voice identity is the whole reason for cloning;
the open-source attempts failed precisely on identity. Do not lower it to "clean up" an
artifact — fix the text instead.

**Stability at 75% buys consistency.** Raising it makes delivery more uniform and more
monotone; lowering it makes it more expressive and less predictable. 75% was chosen for
course narration, where sounding the same in lesson 40 as in lesson 1 matters more than
peak expressiveness.

### Clone Strength, and why low is right

This is the least intuitive setting and the easiest to get wrong, because the name
suggests more is better. It is the opposite.

ElevenLabs describes it as how closely the output follows the clone's **training
recordings**: at lower values *"the voice is smoothed slightly and the delivery gains
more performance flexibility, which often sounds better overall while still sounding
like the original voice"*; at higher values the output stays closer to the recordings
**with less smoothing**. Their guidance is to **start at 0%**, and to raise it only for
voices with distinctive traits or unusual accents.

Measured on a real pt-BR Professional Voice Clone, by ear:

| Value | Result |
|---|---|
| 0% | good, but a few details drifted from the real voice |
| **10%** | **the chosen setting — identity recovered, smoothing kept** |
| above 10% | degrades quickly, and badly |

The cliff above 10% is explained by the same documentation: v4 reproduces the source
faithfully *including its flaws*, and ElevenLabs notes that clipping, plosives and
uneven EQ heard in output "are very likely in the training data". Clone Strength is the
dial that decides how much of the recording's own imperfection comes through. Low keeps
the smoothing that hides it.

**So: start at 0%, try 10%, and treat anything above that as a problem to justify.**
Re-tune per voice — this number came from one voice and one recording session, and a
voice trained in a different room may want something else.

In the API this is `clone_strength` (0 to 1) inside `voice_settings`, on Text to Speech
and Text to Dialogue. It applies only to a PVC fine-tuned for a v4 model. Useful
detail: a v4 request for such a voice uses the voice's **saved** `clone_strength` when
the request sets neither it nor `use_pvc_as_ivc` — so saving 0.1 on the voice makes
every later request inherit it.

## Settings per delivery mode

`narration-script.md` defines the three delivery modes. The e-learning row is the
measured, approved one; **the other two are directions to explore by ear, not
measurements.** Change one value at a time and listen.

| Mode | Stability | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **e-learning** | **75% (validated)** | consistency across dozens of lessons matters more than peak expressiveness |
| conversational | try lower, around 60% | more variation reads as talking rather than presenting |
| how-to | keep 75% or higher | steady and predictable, so a viewer can follow along while doing it |

Similarity stays at 100% and Clone Strength stays where that voice was tuned, in every
mode. They are voice identity, not delivery knobs.

## The recording script shapes the clone

This is worth knowing before cloning a second voice, because it is not obvious:
**"the speaking style in the samples you provide will be replicated in the output."**
ElevenLabs recommends one consistent style per voice, and professional cloning wants
at least 30 minutes of audio, an hour or more preferably, ideally close to three hours.

Their sample recording scripts are documented in three categories — **narrative,
conversational and advertising** — and the web UI additionally groups educational use
cases (how-to, tutorials, e-learning). You can also upload your own script.

The practical consequence:

- A voice cloned from **teaching** audio performs e-learning and how-to narration
  naturally, and sounds slightly formal when handed a loose conversational script.
- A voice cloned from **conversational** audio does the opposite.
- If the user produces both paid course lessons and casual YouTube content and cares
  about the difference, the highest-fidelity answer is **two cloned voices**, each
  recorded with the matching script category. One voice plus a register and settings
  shift is the cheaper answer and is usually good enough — try it first.

Two things the clone copies that are not style: **background noise and recording
artifacts**. A reference recorded in an untreated room produces a voice with that room
in it, on every lesson. And training audio must be spoken, never sung.

## Model limits

| Model | Characters per request |
|---|---|
| **Eleven v4** | **10,000** |
| Eleven v3 | 5,000 |
| Eleven Multilingual v2 | 10,000 |
| Eleven Flash v2.5 | 40,000 |

At ~934 characters per minute of pt-BR narration, 10,000 characters is about
**10 minutes of audio in a single request**. A 2–5 minute lesson is 1,900–4,700
characters, so **one lesson is always one request**. There is no need to split a lesson
for the model's sake, and splitting is undesirable: two requests mean two takes, and
takes do not match seamlessly.

Eleven v4 does **not** support SSML break tags. Pauses come from punctuation —
see `narration-script.md`.

## Whose voice is this?

**The skill does not know the creator's voice, and must never guess one.** This matters
because the skill is shared: the person running it today is not the person it was built
for.

**This is a hard gate.** Ask before the first generation of a course, every time, even
when a voice name appears somewhere in the conversation or in an old `roadmap.md`.
Generating a whole lesson in the wrong voice spends credits on audio nobody can use,
and the skill has no way to tell a stock voice from the creator's own by listening.

1. **Ask, or look.** If a connection exists (below), list the voices on the account and
   look for one the creator cloned — a Professional Voice Clone appears alongside the
   stock voices. On the REST API that is `GET /v1/voices`. Without a connection, ask:
   *"qual é o nome da sua voz na ElevenLabs?"*
2. **If they have one**, use it and record its name in the course's `roadmap.md`, so
   every later lesson uses the same one. Two voices across one course is jarring.
3. **If they have none**, do not silently fall back. Say what the two options are and
   let them choose:
   - **Clone their own voice** — the better result by a wide margin, and the reason this
     pipeline exists. Professional cloning starts on the Creator plan and wants 30
     minutes of audio minimum, an hour or more preferably. Point them at the recording
     script guidance above and wait.
   - **Use a stock voice** — immediate, free of a cloning step, and fine for a draft or
     for a course the creator does not want their own voice on. Say plainly that it will
     not sound like them.
4. **Never create an account, buy a plan, or clone a voice on their behalf.** Those are
   theirs to do.

## Connecting to ElevenLabs

Three routes. They are not alternatives of equal standing — adopt them in this order,
and know what each one can and cannot do.

**The API does not cost more than the web app.** This is worth stating because people
reasonably assume the opposite, and that assumption pushes them into a lot of manual
copy-and-paste for nothing. ElevenLabs is explicit: a request sent to an API endpoint
with your API key *"will deduct credits from your account"* — the same subscription
credits the web app spends, at the same rate per character. Most endpoints are
available on every plan, the free one included. For Flash v2.5 the API is actually
**cheaper**, at 50% lower price per character than a web generation; Eleven v4, which
this pipeline uses, is charged the same either way.

So the choice between these routes is about capability and effort, never about cost.

### 1. The web app, copy and paste — start here, always

The creator pastes `narration/aula.txt` into the ElevenLabs web app, generates, listens,
and downloads the MP3. No connection, no credentials, nothing to set up.

This is slower per lesson and deliberately so: it is how the settings get tuned against
the creator's own ear before any of it is automated, and it is the route that works on
day one for someone who has just been handed this skill. Produce the script, hand it
over, and wait for the audio.

Ask them to drop the download at `<lesson-dir>/audio/aula.mp3` and tell you when it is
there. Do not proceed to the video on an assumed filename.

**What it costs you:** no word timings. Alignment has to be estimated from the audio
(`align-wav.mjs`), which drifts on long pauses.

### 2. The hosted MCP server — generation from inside the conversation

ElevenLabs runs a hosted MCP server at **`https://api.elevenlabs.io/v1/mcp`**. It uses
OAuth, so no API key is ever copied, pasted or stored. Text to Speech is one of its
scopes: it generates speech and returns a short-lived download link.

From Claude Code:

```bash
claude mcp add --transport http elevenlabs https://api.elevenlabs.io/v1/mcp
```

Then run `/mcp` inside a session to complete the OAuth sign-in. In Claude Desktop it is
a connector in the connectors directory — connect and sign in there.

Workspaces in an isolated data-residency region use a regional URL instead (EU, India,
Singapore — for example `https://api.eu.residency.elevenlabs.io/v1/mcp`), and must sign
in with the account for that region.

**What it costs you:** the documented capabilities centre on agent management plus text
to speech; **listing or cloning voices is not documented as available**, so voice
discovery may still need the REST API or a question to the creator. And it returns
audio, not timings.

### 3. The REST API — the only route that gives exact timings

Standard text-to-speech returns audio only. The endpoint worth using is
**`POST /v1/text-to-speech/{voice_id}/with-timestamps`**, which returns the audio plus
character-level timing:

```json
{
  "audio_base64": "...",
  "alignment": {
    "characters": ["O", " ", "H", "P", "A", ...],
    "character_start_times_seconds": [0.0, 0.07, 0.12, ...],
    "character_end_times_seconds":   [0.07, 0.12, 0.19, ...]
  }
}
```

This matters more than it looks: those timings are **exact**, so captions and reveals
land on the right frame, where the estimated alignment drifts. Sync is the product, so
this endpoint is the reason to move off copy-and-paste once the settings are settled.
`scripts/studio/timestamps-to-srt.mjs` turns that alignment into the SRT the pipeline
already reads. `GET /v1/voices` on the same key answers the voice question above.

Timestamps are API only — neither the web app nor the MCP server exposes them.

**Credential handling, not negotiable:** the API key is the creator's. Read it from the
environment (`ELEVENLABS_API_KEY`), never write it into a file in the repo, never echo
it, and never ask them to paste it into the conversation. If no key is present, say so
and stay on route 1 or 2 — do not block a lesson on it.

### Not this: the local MCP package

`uvx elevenlabs-mcp` with an API key in the config is **deprecated by ElevenLabs in
favour of the hosted server**, and the repository is no longer actively maintained. Do
not set it up for someone new.

## When a video needs more than one take

One take per video is always better: a single generation has one continuous energy and
no seam. Everything up to the 10,000-character request limit — about 10 minutes — fits
in one, so most work never needs splitting.

When a video genuinely exceeds it, split the **script**, not the request, and split it
well:

- **Cut at a structural boundary**, between segments of the outline, where the script
  already has a paragraph break and the delivery already pauses. Never mid-argument,
  and never mid-paragraph.
- **Keep every setting identical** across the segments — same voice, same stability,
  same clone strength, same model. Changing one between takes makes the seam obvious.
- **Carry the prosody across the cut.** On the API, pass the neighbouring segments in
  `previous_text` and `next_text`. The model uses them for context without speaking
  them, so the delivery continues across the join instead of restarting. This is the
  single best mitigation for a seam, and it is **API only** — a web-app split has no
  equivalent, which is one more reason to connect.
- **Expect a small discontinuity anyway.** Generation is nondeterministic, so two takes
  differ slightly in energy and pace even with identical settings. Pass a fixed `seed`
  for more consistent results, and put the cut where a pause belongs so the difference
  reads as a breath rather than an edit.
- **Generate them in order, one at a time**, and listen across each join before moving on.

The segments stay separate files all the way to the render — `video-pipeline.md` has
why they are assembled in the composition rather than stitched by hand afterwards.

## Generate one lesson at a time

**Never generate a batch of lessons in parallel.** This is a money rule, not a tidiness
rule: characters are charged when the request is made, not when the result is approved,
so every failed, truncated or simply disliked take is paid for. Firing ten lessons at
once and finding a problem in the first one means paying for nine takes that get
thrown away.

The loop is: generate one → check its integrity → listen → keep it → next. Scripts are
written as a batch so the creator can read the course as a whole; audio never is.

Before generating a module, tell the creator its total character count, so the spend is
agreed rather than discovered.

**Regenerating the exact same thing is free, up to twice.** ElevenLabs allows up to two
free regenerations per generation, but only for *identical content and parameters*.
Change one word, or nudge stability, and it is a new paid generation. That makes the
tuning loop more expensive than it looks: trying four stability values on one lesson
costs four generations, not one. Tune on a short excerpt, not on a full lesson.

## After the audio exists

Measure before building anything on it:

```bash
ffprobe -v error -show_entries format=duration -of csv=p=0 audio/aula.mp3
```

Then check integrity, because a bad take renders into a perfectly valid-looking video:

| Check | How | Fail means |
|---|---|---|
| Real duration | ffprobe vs. the character budget | wildly short = truncated generation |
| Truncation | listen to the last 3 seconds / check the tail is not mid-word | regenerate |
| Silence holes | `silencedetect=noise=-35dB:d=1.0` | a hole over ~1.5s mid-lesson is a defect |
| Clipping | `volumedetect`, `max_volume` should be below 0 dB | regenerate or lower output gain |

A lesson that fails any of these gets regenerated, not patched. Never apply a limiter
or `loudnorm` to fix a level problem — compression flattens consonant transients and
the voice goes muffled. Use plain gain if a level change is genuinely needed.

## Cost, so a course can be budgeted

Credits are charged **per character**, from one pool shared by every ElevenLabs product
and by both the web app and the API alike. The measured rate is ~934 characters per
minute of audio. Cost per hour of finished narration, by plan:

| Plan | US$/month | included audio | **US$/hour of audio** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Starter | 6 | ~32 min | 11.21 |
| Creator | 22 | ~130 min | 10.19 |
| Pro | 99 | ~642 min | 9.25 |
| Scale | 299 | ~1,927 min | 9.31 |

A 4-hour course is about **240 minutes of audio**, or ~224,000 credits — roughly two
months of the Creator plan, about US$ 41, spread over however long it is produced in. Professional voice cloning starts on the
Creator plan; instant cloning is available from Starter. Credits are a single pool
shared across all ElevenLabs products, so generating music or dubbing eats the
narration budget.

Tell the user the character count of a module before generating it, so there are no
surprises mid-course.
